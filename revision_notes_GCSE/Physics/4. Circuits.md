## Static Charge:

![[Pasted image 20261109182405.png]]

Charging by friction - 2 materials rubbed together can transfer electrons. The one losing electrons becomes positively charged and the other negatively.
Charging by induction - a charged object is placed near a conductor. The charged object will attract or repel electrons based on its charge. This *induces* a charge in the second object.

## Earthing & Sparking:

Sparking occurs when enough charge (potential difference) builds up between 2 objects.
--> this causes the transfer of negative electrons, balancing the charge

Earthing is charge flowing from an object to the ground, balancing the charge.

#### Dangers & Uses:

Spray painting uses static charge to evenly coat a surface. The paint is attracted to the object due to the difference in charge. This minimises wastage.

Photocopiers use static to attract toner onto a drum and roll it off onto the sheet of paper.

On aeroplanes, air moving quickly over a wing can cause a buildup of charge, proving dangerous when refuelling if not properly earthed.

Lightning is also a form of static. Strikes can cause wildfires, damage property and even kill.

## Electrical Circuits:

*Current* - rate of flow of charge, measured in Coulombs (C)

$$current = \dfrac{charge\ flow}{time}$$
$$Q = It$$
![[Pasted image 20261109184250.png]]


## Current in Series & Parallel:

In series, amperage/current stays constant throughout the circuit. In parallel, charge is split in the ratio of resistance. 
--> the current into a junction must equal the current out of the junction

## Measuring PD:

*Potential difference* - a measure of energy, per unit of charge. Measured in joules per coulomb or volts (V).

Volts are measured with Voltmeters.
--> these are placed in parallel and have a very high resistance (no current flow)
--> in series, PD is spilt across all components, adding to the total of the power supply

## Resistance:

*Resistance* - the opposition a component has to the flow of current. Measured in ohms (Ω).

#### Ohm's law:
$$potential\ difference\ =\ current\ *\ resistance$$
$$V = IR$$
#### I/V in a resistor:

Current through a resistor is directly proportional to voltage. This makes it an ohmic resistor.

![[Pasted image 20261109191308.png]]

#### I/V in a bulb:

In a bulb, higher voltages increase resistance due to the increase in temperature. This is because the particles vibrate faster, making electrons more likely to encounter other electrons and atoms in their path.

![[Pasted image 20261109191449.png]]

#### I/V in a diode:

A diode only allows current to flow in 1 direction. The resistance is very high at low voltages, decreasing as PD increases.

![[Pasted image 20261109191047.png]]

#### Other Resistors:

*Thermistor* - a resistor where resistance decreases with temperature (negative coefficient thermistor).
*Light Dependant Resistor (LDR)* - a resistor where resistance decreases with greater brightness.

## Power & Energy:

Current transfers energy from the power source (chemical, normally) to the components.

*Power* - "energy per second", measured in joules per second or watts (W)

$$power\ =\ \dfrac{energy\ transfered}{time}$$
